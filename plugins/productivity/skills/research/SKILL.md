# Research

A research answer is only as good as the evidence chain under it. Work from
original evidence, count only independent sources as corroboration, and keep
what was measured apart from what anyone concludes from it.

## 1. Decide what kind of question this is

Most requests are one shape; some are two (a decision that rests on a
contested claim). Pick the shapes that fit and apply only their extras.

| The user wants… | Shape | Extras the answer must carry |
|---|---|---|
| A claim, quote or meme checked | **Fact-check** | Split into testable assertions. Label each *supported / partly supported / unsupported / contradicted / unverifiable*. End with an accurate rewrite that keeps the uncertainty. |
| A statistic or headline explained | **Trace** | Follow it to the original source. State date, population, sample, definitions, how it was calculated, the baseline, and absolute vs relative change. End with one accurate, shareable sentence. |
| Sources to read | **Reading list** | 8–12 sources, original research and official data first. Table of date, relevant finding, limitation. Flag sources that rest on the same evidence. Recommend the 3 most useful and say why. |
| One paper or report understood | **Paper** | Methods, sample, key numbers, limitations in plain English, citing sections. End with what it supports, what it cannot establish, and who it applies to. |
| Sources set against each other | **Compare** | Table of claims, methods, populations, limitations. Explain each disagreement as definitions, timing, or design. Evidence-weighted conclusion and what stays unresolved. |
| A contested debate mapped | **Debate** | Each side's strongest evidence *and* strongest counterevidence. Separate factual disagreements from value judgments. Name the finding that would most change the picture. |
| Why something happens | **Cause** | 2–4 candidate causes with mechanism, supporting and conflicting evidence. Correlation vs causation called out. Rank by evidence; walk the strongest mechanism step by step. |
| Help making a decision | **Decide** | 2–4 realistic options with tradeoffs against the user's stated priorities. Findings and your judgment in separate sections. A conditional recommendation, what would change it, the next 3 actions. |
| A broad topic surveyed | **Survey** | The 3–5 questions that matter most, answered in order of importance. Close with the open questions the field has not settled. |

If the scope is too broad to answer well in one pass, or the user explicitly asks
for a plan, give a **research plan** instead: 5 answerable questions ranked by
importance, the evidence each needs, the assumptions you are making, an ordered
checklist. Ask before spending a long search on an ambiguous request.

## 2. Gather evidence

- **Go upstream.** For every load-bearing claim, reach the original study,
  dataset, official statistic or primary document. A news story, blog,
  encyclopedia or press release is a pointer to the source, not the source.
  If you cannot reach the original, say so next to the claim.
- **Check independence.** Several articles reporting the same study are one
  piece of evidence. Name the underlying source and count it once.
- **Look for the other side.** Run at least one search aimed at evidence
  against your emerging conclusion.
- **Check dates.** Prefer the latest evidence. Flag anything superseded.
- **Scale effort to stakes.** A trivia check may need two searches; a decision
  someone will act on needs as many as it takes to reach primary evidence for
  each load-bearing claim.

## 3. Write the answer

In this order:

1. **Bottom line**: the answer in 1–3 sentences, with your confidence.
2. **The shape's extras** from the table above.
3. **Findings**: each with its source and evidence quality (RCT, meta-analysis,
   observational, survey, expert opinion, anecdote). Write what was measured
   apart from how anyone interpreted it.
4. **Limits**: what you could not verify, where the evidence is thin, and who
   the findings may not apply to.
5. **Sources**: primary sources first; mark secondary ones as secondary.

Match length to the question. A one-line claim gets a short answer that still
has all five parts.
